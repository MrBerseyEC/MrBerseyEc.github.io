Denary is the number system we use every day. It has ten digits, 0 to 9, and each place is worth 10 times the one to its right. Binary works in exactly the same way, but with two digits, 0 and 1, and each place is worth 2 times the one to its right.

Place values

In denary the place values are powers of 10. In binary they are powers of 2. Type a denary number to see how it is built from its place values.

Binary to denary

Click the bits to switch them between 0 and 1. Add up the place values of every column that contains a 1.

The bit on the far left has the highest place value and is called the most significant bit (MSB). The bit on the far right is the least significant bit (LSB) and is always worth 1. The MSB of an n-bit number is worth 2n−1, so in a byte it is worth 27 = 128.

Denary to binary: divide by 2

Keep dividing by 2 and writing down the remainder until you reach 0. Then read the remainders from the bottom up.

If the answer has to be 8 bits long, fill the empty places on the left with 0s. These leading zeros do not change the value: 1101 and 0000 1101 are both 13.

Denary to binary: place values

Another way is to work from the left. If the place value fits into what is left, write a 1 and subtract it. If it does not fit, write a 0.
