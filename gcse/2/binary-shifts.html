A binary shift moves every bit in a pattern a number of places to the left or to the right. Bits that fall off the end are discarded and the empty places are filled in. Shifting is a very fast way for a processor to multiply and divide by powers of 2.

The four shifts

ShiftBits moveDiscardedEmpty places filled withEffect on a number
Logical shift leftleftthe leftmost bits0s on the rightMultiplies an unsigned integer by 2 for each place
Logical shift rightrightthe rightmost bits0s on the leftDivides an unsigned integer by 2 for each place
Arithmetic shift leftleftthe leftmost bits0s on the rightExactly the same as a logical shift left
Arithmetic shift rightrightthe rightmost bitscopies of the original MSB on the leftDivides a two's complement integer by 2 for each place, keeping its sign

A logical shift treats every bit the same way, whatever the pattern represents. An arithmetic shift right takes account of the MSB so that a negative two's complement number stays negative. Shifting by n places multiplies or divides by 2n: one place is 2, two places is 4, three places is 8.

When shifting gives the wrong answer

ProblemExampleWhy
Loss of precision
shifting right		0010 0001 (33)
→ 0001 0000 (16)		33 ÷ 2 is 16.5, but the 1 in the rightmost place was discarded, so the result is rounded down. This happens whenever a 1 is shifted off the right-hand end.
Overflow
shifting left		1001 0000 (144)
→ 0010 0000 (32)		144 × 2 is 288, which does not fit in 8 bits. A 1 was shifted off the left-hand end, so the result is wrong.
Sign lost
shifting a negative number left		1011 (−5)
→ 0110 (+6)		A left shift does not preserve the MSB. In 4 bits, the bit worth −8 has been discarded, so the answer is +6 instead of −10. Left shifts are not used to multiply negative numbers.
