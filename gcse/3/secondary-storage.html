Secondary storage

RAM is volatile: it loses everything when the power goes off. Secondary storage is non-volatile, so it keeps programs and files permanently. It stores the same 0s and 1s in three very different physical ways.

How the 0s and 1s are stored

Type a character (or click the bits), choose a technology, then write the data and read it back.

  • A hard disk drive (HDD) has spinning platters coated in a magnetic material.
  • A read/write head on a moving arm magnetises tiny areas of the surface in one of two directions: one direction represents 1, the other 0.
  • To read, the head senses the direction of each area as it passes underneath.
  • Moving parts make it slower to access, noisier and easier to damage than solid state, but it is cheap for very large capacities. Magnetic tape works the same way, for backups.

  • CDs, DVDs and Blu-ray discs store data on a spiral track of pits (dips) and lands (flat areas).
  • To read, a laser shines on the track. A land reflects the light straight back to a sensor; a pit scatters it, so less light returns.
  • To write a recordable disc, a stronger laser changes the dye layer to make the equivalent of pits.
  • Cheap and portable, but low capacity, slow, and easily scratched.

Simplification: here a land is 1 and a pit is 0. On real discs, a change from pit to land (or land to pit) represents a 1, and no change represents a 0.

  • Solid state drives (SSDs), USB sticks and memory cards use flash memory made of millions of tiny cells (transistors).
  • Each cell has a floating gate that can trap electrons. A cell with trapped electrons reads as 0; an empty cell reads as 1.
  • Cells must be erased (emptied) a whole block at a time before they can be written again, which is why each cell can only be rewritten a limited number of times.
  • No moving parts: very fast, silent, low power and hard to damage, but more expensive per GB.
