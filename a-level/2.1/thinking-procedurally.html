Thinking procedurally means breaking a problem down into smaller parts, working out the order in which the steps must happen, and deciding which parts should become sub-procedures. Each part can then be designed, written and tested separately.

Breaking a problem down

In top-down design, a problem is broken into its major tasks, and each task is broken down again until every part is simple enough to be written as a single subroutine. The result is drawn as a structure chart, also called a hierarchy chart: an upside-down tree with the whole problem at the top. Click a dashed box to break it down further.

A structure chart shows which parts make up the solution, not the order of every instruction, and it has no loops or decisions. Reading the bottom boxes from left to right gives the subroutines that need writing. Try drawing the chart on paper for each problem before you reveal it.

From components to sub-procedures

  
Each component becomes a sub-procedure with a name that says what it does. Lines 1 to 4 and lines 5 to 8 of the original did the same job with a different prompt, so they become one function that is called twice. The part that differs is passed in as a parameter.

  

    

    

      
Why sub-procedures matter

      
    
        
  • The main program reads like a summary of the solution: four lines in place of fourteen.
    • 
        
  • Code that is needed more than once is written once and called with different arguments.
    • 
        
  • Each sub-procedure can be tested on its own, and a fault is easier to find.
    • 
        
  • A change is made in one place. Raising the limit of 10 tickets means editing only getTickets.
    • 
        
  • Different programmers can write different sub-procedures at the same time.
    • 
        
  • A self-contained sub-procedure can be reused in another program.
    • 
        
  • The caller needs to know only the interface: the name, the parameters and their order, and what is returned.
